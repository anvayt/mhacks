How it works

The question: what will this apartment cost to heat and cool, season by season? The answer is one number per season (a point estimate) for heating and for cooling, in dollars and in energy (gas ccf, electric kWh). Every figure in this section is read live from the model's result files on the server (model/results/*.json), so it updates after a rebuild.

  1. Find the building. The address is geocoded, matched to the city's building footprint, and, for an apartment complex, grouped with the complex's other buildings (by the name the city gives it). We also check whether it is one of the – Ann Arbor residential properties that report real monthly utility meter readings to the city.
  2. Describe it. Floor area, year built, stories, height and envelope shape (how much wall and roof per unit of volume). Values come from the city's benchmarking report when the building has one. Otherwise from footprints, plus Census data for the neighborhood (median year built of rentals, and whether homes there mostly heat with gas or electricity).
  3. Get the weather at that exact spot. Heating and cooling are driven by degree-days: how far, and for how many days, the outdoor temperature sits below 65 °F (heating) or above it (cooling). Daily temperatures from a weather reanalysis are shifted month by month to match PRISM's 800 m temperature grid at the building. This works for a typical year (1991–2020 normals), any past year, or the next 12 months (forecast).
  4. Estimate energy with the best evidence available. If the building has real meters, we fit its own "energy vs degree-days" line (the Princeton Scorekeeping Method) and read off how much gas goes to heat and how much electricity goes to cooling. If not, two models predict how much heat per degree-day a building like this uses, and we average them: one is trained on the metered Ann Arbor buildings, the other on 18,756 simulated Michigan homes (NREL ResStock) calibrated to those meters. Small buildings (houses, duplexes) aren't covered by the city's meters, so they use the ResStock model alone, which renter answers can sharpen (window panes, floor, cooling type…).
  5. Price it. Each month's energy is priced with Michigan residential prices from the US Energy Information Administration. Gas uses the marginal price (without the fixed monthly charge), because heating is extra gas on top of what you'd use anyway.
  6. Add up the seasons (winter = Dec–Feb, spring = Mar–May, summer = Jun–Aug, fall = Sep–Nov), and scale the building's total to your unit by floor area.
